# No stars, no red X, and no "you are so smart"

*29 September 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-09-29-no-stars-no-red-x.vi.md)*

Most learning games give stars and points, show a red mark for a wrong answer, and praise the child: "Great job, you are so smart!" Before we copied this, we looked at what it does to children.

## What we found

- **Expected rewards lower interest, most of all in children.** Across 128 experiments, rewards that people expected for a task lowered their later interest in it (d = −0.36), and more for children than for college students. Feedback that gave information about how well a person did raised it (d = +0.66) ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)).
- **Praise for being smart harms.** After "you're smart", children in grade 5 chose easier tasks, solved fewer problems after a failure, and 38% lied about their scores, against 13% after praise for their effort ([Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)).
- **Feedback about the person often does damage.** About one third of all feedback made performance worse, and that was feedback about the person: praise, grades, and comparison ([Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://www.winginstitute.org/does-feedback-improve-performance)).
- **A failure must be cheap and private.** Players of Celeste kept trying because a new try came at once and "only you can see how many times you died" ([IJHCS 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581923002082)).
- **Easier is better than it sounds.** Children who practiced at 90% success did more problems and learned more than children at 75% or 60% ([Jansen et al. 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951)).
- **A session ends best by the device, at a natural stop.** A warning from a parent two minutes before the end made the end harder for children under six ([Hiniker et al. 2016](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/ScreenTimeTantrums.pdf)). That study had small children only, so for older children we take it as likely, not proven.

## What we decided

- **The world speaks, never a grader.** No "wrong", no red X, no praise, and no comparison with other children. The plank falls short, the band snaps, and the child sees why.
- **A new try is one tap and takes less than two seconds,** and nobody sees how many tries a child made.
- **Rewards give information or come as a surprise,** never as a contract: "You used three planks; last week you used five." No row of stars shows before a task.
- **Tasks aim at 80 to 90% success on the first try.** A harder version is on offer for pride, and it is never forced.
- **A lost raid takes some rice,** never a thing that the child built and never a friend. In grade 1, a raid takes nothing.
- **A session ends inside the story,** at a natural stop: the sun sets, and the hero goes home to rest. There is no countdown.

So the people of Tre never say "giỏi quá". They say what changed: "Hôm trước cháu đếm từng cây; hôm nay cháu nhìn hàng là biết."
