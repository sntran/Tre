# A view that grows with the child

*11 October 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-10-11-a-view-that-grows.vi.md)*

On a phone, the hero of Tre was small: about 8 mm high on the glass. In the play tests, children of six and seven often said "I can't see me" and "the sticks are tiny". We made the view closer for everyone. Then I asked: why one view for all? In the game Evoland, the camera and the look of the game change as you play. Can the view of Tre change with the age of the child, so that a child of five and a child of twelve each get the view that helps them?

## What we found

- **Evoland changes with the story, not with the player.** The game starts as a 2D game of four colors and becomes a 3D world as you go on. The changes are a joke about the history of video games, for players who remember those games. They do not depend on who plays.
- **The hands change with age, through all of school.** Researchers who studied children and adults on phones and tablets found that the younger the child, the bigger the targets must be. Children of 7 to 10 missed about 30 in 100 targets of 6.4 mm at the first try, and many more of their taps went near the last target in place of the new one (Anthony and others 2013). Children of 5 to 10 missed more targets than adults, and children of 5 and 6 were the most slowed by a busy game screen (Woodward and others 2016).
- **The eyes change less than we thought.** At 7 to 10, children search an area of the screen as large as adults do (Dye and Bavelier 2010). But they follow fewer moving things at a time (about 3, against about 4 for adults, in the same study). In a test with letters, children of 5, 8, and 11 needed about two and a half times more space between things than adults, with no change from 5 to 11 (Jeon and others 2010). So a close view helps all children of primary school, and most of all the youngest.
- **Age is only a first guess.** Children of one age are very different. But a few taps tell a lot: from a run of 7 to 15 taps, a computer program could tell a child of 3 to 6 from a young adult almost every time (Vatavu, Anthony, and Brown 2015).

## What we decided

- **The view fits the grade.** The hero is about 13 mm high on a phone for Pre-K and K, 11 mm for grades 1 and 2, 10 mm for grades 3 to 5, and 8.5 mm from grade 6. A pinch still shows a far view, and each task still frames its own work.
- **The parent can change it.** A setting in the parent area chooses a nearer or a farther view. The page of the week says so when many of the child's taps miss, and suggests a nearer view. The game never changes the view by itself.
- **No wider view as a prize.** Evoland gives new looks as rewards. A wider view as a prize takes the close view away from a child who still needs it, so the view changes only with the grade or the setting, between plays.

## The limit of what we know

The research measured taps on targets, not the size of a hero in a game, so the four sizes are our first guess. Our play testers so far are in grades 1 and 2. We will test the other sizes with younger and older children, and change them if the misses say so. The details are in issue #84.
