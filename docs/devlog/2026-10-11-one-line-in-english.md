# One line in English

*11 October 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-10-11-one-line-in-english.vi.md)*

Many of the children who play Tre were born outside Vietnam. They understand spoken Vietnamese from their parents, but they read English better. In a play test, Tí, a boy of seven, played in Vietnamese because his mom asked him to. At a hard task he said: "These words are hard. Maybe in English I'd understand better." He looked for a language button in the menu, and there was none. Only a parent could change the language, behind a lock.

So we asked: should a child be able to change the language in the game?

## What we found

- **Help in the stronger language helps understanding.** Dutch children who watched English programs with Dutch subtitles learned more English words than children with no subtitles (Koolstra and Beentjes 1999). A review of 26 studies found that the meaning of a word in the stronger language helps more than an explanation in the language that the learner is learning (Kim, Lee, and Lee).
- **A free choice often goes one way.** In a large survey of children of immigrants in the United States, 72 in 100 preferred English, and only about 5 in 100 children of Vietnamese families spoke both languages well, against 27 in 100 for all groups (Portes and Hao). In a small study of a book app with a language button, 8 of 12 heritage children of four and five chose the majority language. One said: "I understand Dutch better" (Bus, Broekhof, and Vaessen 2023).
- **Children's choices change the family.** In case studies of families, the parents often followed the language of the child (Fogle and King).

## What we decided

- **One line in the other language, when the child asks.** Each line of a person has a small button. A tap says that one line in the other language, and then says it again in the language of the game. The child understands, and the game stays in the language of the family.
- **A switch in the menu.** The child can change the whole game from the menu, as the owner of the game asked.
- **The parent decides.** The parent can turn off the switch or the line button, and the parent page shows how often the child used them.

## The limit of what we know

No study that we found tested a game like this with heritage children. The studies of subtitles and word meanings were with older learners or with English as the target language, and the study of the book app was small. We will count in the play tests how often children tap the line button, and whether they still listen to the Vietnamese. The details are in issue #82.
