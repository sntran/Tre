# Heard but not seen

*7 November 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-11-07-heard-but-not-seen.vi.md)*

In this play test, seven children of K to grade 5 played after #155–#163. Many things got better: no raid starts in the dark, the sleep brings the morning with a drum and a rooster, the coach holds the raid while he shows, and a boy of grade 3 who lost the river in the last play won it this time ("I did the river game this time!"). A girl of grade 5 said that the word lines "helped a lot". But again and again the game said one thing and showed another. The voice said "rest until the morning", and the bed was not on the button. The voice said that the iron horse was done, and there was no horse. The trap and the slingshot used the same count words for two different rules. Our question for this round: when does a child believe what the game says?

## What we found

- **A word with no thing to tap.** At night every person said "Trời tối rồi. Mình nghỉ đến sáng.", but the star of rest did not show while the step had a person, the button never showed the sleep on Nghé, and near Gióng the mat and the talk changed places at each of his steps. A girl of grade 1 used about 60 touches to find the sleep. Norman (2013) calls the sign of a possible act a signifier: an act with no signifier that the user sees is an act that the user does not find.
- **One button, two rules.** At K the coach first taught the slingshot: "the post of the soldier, that many presses". Then the villager asked for a trap at post 2. Four children of K to grade 2 pressed two times, and the trap went to the nearest post, post 1. They were right to do it: a young child counts one word for each thing, and the last word is the number (Gelman and Gallistel 1978). The game was wrong to use the same words for another rule. The line also said "cột nhất", which is not Vietnamese, because two blocks of keys had the same names.
- **A cut that is not at the mark.** At the bamboo staffs the mark goes up with the time of the world, but the cut used the time of the finger. On a slow device the world runs slower than the clock, so the cut is higher than the mark. A boy of K got six rings, two, six, three, and never four, and stopped. Shneiderman (1983) put "operations whose impact on the object of interest is immediately visible" at the center of direct manipulation. What the child sees at the release must be what she gets. The show of the woodcutter also cut at three rings while the sample had four, and young children copy what an adult shows (Lyons, Young, and Keil 2007).
- **A button that changes in the middle of the work.** At bà lang, with empty hands at the basket, the big button gives the basket. So after each put, the next press is the give, and a boy of grade 4 gave the basket two times by mistake: "The big button changes what it does and I don't notice." Norman (1981) calls this a mode error.
- **A mistake with no next step.** Ten sacks of rice for three mats: a boy of grade 2 put the extra sack on a mat, and Nghé said "Chiếu của Nghé nhiều hơn." again and again. No line said that the extra stays on the pile. He carried sacks for about 100 commands and stopped: "nobody tells me what to do with the extra one." Two other children left the extra on the pile by themselves and liked the share. Frydman and Bryant (1988) found that young children share by dealing; Hattie and Timperley (2007) found that feedback helps when it says where to go next.
- **Said, not shown.** "Ngựa sắt đã xong!" and no horse at the forge; a boy of grade 3 tapped a grey table. "Xèo! Hơi nước bay lên" and no steam. "Gióng cao thêm một đầu" in a far view. A voice of bà lang while she was not on the screen. The voice read a key aloud: "Đi tìm npc.giong-sky.name." Mayer (2009) found that people learn better when words and the pictures that they name come at the same time. When they do not, a child learns that the voice and the world do not match.
- **Grown-up words.** After the raid of Trâu Sơn, a girl of grade 1 heard "Năm 2010, UNESCO ghi danh hội Gióng là di sản văn hóa phi vật thể của nhân loại.": "I don't know these grown-up words." Beck, McKeown, and Kucan (2013) teach a new word at its use, in words that the child knows. The word lines of this round did that well, and the lines of history did not.

## What we decided

- **At night the bed is the next step** (#164): the star of rest for every step, and the sleep first on the button, also on Nghé.
- **One press, one post, for the trap and the slingshot** (#165), with the trap first and count words for the posts; **each press says its count, the post of the enemy glows, and a miss says the count and the place** (#166).
- **The extra sack goes back to the pile, and the line says so** (#167); each mat shows its owner and lies on open ground.
- **The cut is at the mark on the screen**, the voice counts the rings, and a break says where to let go (#168).
- **The big button stays the work**; the basket is given after a tap on bà lang (#169).
- **What the voice says, the screen shows** (#170): the iron horse, the steam, the growth, the speaker, and the print of each card. **The ride shows** (#171). **Each carry has its next carry on the button** (#172). **No key aloud, and words and history by the grade** (#173).

## The limit of what we know

These were seven plays on slow test phones. The phones had to restart every two hours, so "the phone froze and went back to the start screen" comes from our set-up. The slow phones also made the problem of the staffs bigger than on a real phone, because the cut followed the clock; a slow school tablet has the same problem in a smaller way. A print can take longer to draw on our phones, so some empty cards may not show on a real one. The children were testers who did not know the game, and we heard them through their notes; the counts of touches are from the logs.
