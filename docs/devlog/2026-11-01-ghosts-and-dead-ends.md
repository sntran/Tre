# Ghosts and dead ends

*1 November 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-11-01-ghosts-and-dead-ends.vi.md)*

In this play test, seven children of K to grade 5 played after #146–#154. Many things got better: the ride starts at once, the rest of the rice goes to a farmer beside the mats, the woodcutter's fractions at grade 5 made a girl say "I like that the game showed it fitting 3 times and 4 times", and a girl of grade 1 went from the iron to the forge, to the rice for Gióng, to the moment when he rides out ("Có! Mình đi cùng bạn."). But two kinds of problems stopped the children. Our fix for "in the way" made ghosts. And at some places the game left the child with nothing to do and nobody to say why.

## What we found

- **A pale person is not a person out of the way.** In the last entry we decided that a figure in front of the work goes pale and see-through. In the game, that made piles. At the house of Gióng, three see-through people stood in one place while baby Gióng spoke his first words, and a girl of grade 1 asked "Which one is me?" At the rice, Gióng was pale at the very moment that he grew. Bà lang was a grey ghost for the whole task of a girl of grade 4. Elmqvist and Tsigas (2008) list this risk: see-through layers weaken the cues of depth and add load, and many of them confuse the view. A house can go pale. A person must step aside.
- **A dead end after a loss.** A lost raid comes back at the next dawn. A boy of grade 3 had lost two raids that both waited for the same dawn, and the second ore waited for one of them. No star showed. The elder told him to "tap the star", and there was none. He walked from person to person for about 100 touches and stopped: "I looked everywhere, there is none!" Csikszentmihalyi (1990) puts clear goals first among the conditions of an absorbing activity. A child with no next step leaves.
- **Help that costs the game.** A boy of grade 3 asked for help at the river. The fisher began his example, and the two thuồng luồng swam on and reached the ford while he counted "một". The raid was lost during the help. Wood, Bruner, and Ross (1976) describe good tutoring as "reduction in degrees of freedom": while the tutor shows, the task gets simpler, and the child does not lose because of the help.
- **A press that only says the line again.** A boy of K had a trap in the hands for the whole raid. Each press away from the road said "Bẫy đặt ở cột hai nhé.", 14 times; his taps on post 2 hit the people who stood there. He also had the straw flags and the slingshot in the same raid. Cowan (2016) puts the span of a child of five at about two new things.
- **Silence after a mistake, and a fix with no act.** At the bamboo staffs, a girl of grade 1 never learned to hold the button, and three breaks came with no word from anybody (we checked it again with no browser). At bà lang, the voice dropped lines when a girl of grade 4 pressed fast, so her count was wrong; after the count she could not take the extra out, and later the basket became right with no act of hers, and bà lang praised her. Metcalfe (2017) found that an error helps learning when the learner makes the correction. A fix by the game teaches nothing, and praise after it teaches the wrong thing.
- **Empty pictures.** Many cards of "Tranh mới" and most prints of one notebook were empty boxes; the prints that drew were close crops (a green block for a frog).

## What we decided

- **People step aside; only things go pale** (#158). The hero, the speaker, the person of the task, and Gióng are never pale.
- **The next step always has a star, and the child can sleep until the morning** (#155). A raid and the trip to Trâu Sơn start in the day.
- **An example holds the raid still** (#156), and **up to grade 1 a raid has one tool** (#157). A press with a trap always puts it.
- **A count is on the screen and in each line; a help never fixes the work in silence** (#160). **A task with a hold shows the hold**, and a break has its word (#161).
- **The bend of the smith is an L in a wide view** (#159). **A print always draws, shows the whole thing, and is one for each skill** (#162). **Lines say what is true at the place** (#163).

## The limit of what we know

These were seven plays on slow test phones. The phones had to restart every two hours, so the "froze and went back to the start screen" in the notes is our set-up, not the game. On a slow phone, a quick tap can read as a long press, so the hopscotch errors of one boy may not happen on a real phone; we will check them on one. The timer of the turns of the view runs in real time, and real time ran between the commands of the test, so the turns that two children saw may be fewer on a phone. Elmqvist and Tsigas wrote about the views of data for adults, not about games for children; the piles that we saw are our own evidence.
