# The road east

*30 October 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-10-30-the-road-east.vi.md)*

After Gióng grows, the child goes with him to the soldiers of Ân at the foot of Trâu Sơn, a line of low hills east of Phù Đổng. We let the child find the hills by the clues of the old geography book ([Where is Núi Trâu?](2026-10-03-where-is-nui-trau.md)): until the child comes to the hills, the star of the quest shows only the road, 40 cells ahead of the child. In the play test, a boy of K tapped the star 15 times and walked six minutes of the game in the dark: "I think I'm going in circles!" A boy of grade 2 needed 34 touches. In the play test before, the same boy of K stopped the game on this road. How does a young child find a far place?

## What we found

- **Directions stay hard for a long time.** Yang and Merrill (2020) asked 94 children of 6 to 12 years to point to north, and then to east, in a room with a window. To find north was harder than to know how the directions go around, and many children could not use a frame of the world around them. "Toward the sunrise" asks the same thing, and at night it asks for a sun that is not there. Our boy of K: "But it's night, where's the sun?"
- **Young children learn a route by its turns.** Lingwood, Blades, Farran, Courbois and Matthews (2015) showed children of 5 a route with six turns in a maze on a computer. When the adult named the landmark at each turn on the first walk, the children needed fewer tries to learn the route (0.89 against 2.21 on average) and made fewer errors. A second study taught children to look at landmarks, and that helped on a new route too. The authors cite earlier work: children of 6 look at near landmarks, and children of 12 look for far ones.
- **Our star had no turn and no landmark.** It was a point on an empty road that moved ahead with the child, so each tap ended where nothing was. The two people of the clues stood on the road, but the walk did not stop at them, and at night the boy heard a clue and did not see the person: "who is talking? I don't see anyone."
- **A line that is not true costs more than a long road.** Gióng said that the soldiers were "ngoài cổng làng", outside the village gate. They were about 580 cells away.

## What we decided

- **A tap on the star walks to the next thing on the road:** the next person of the way, who says the clue while the child sees her, and then the foot of the hills. No tap ends on an empty road. About three taps take a child from the gate to the soldiers, and each stop has a person and a line.
- **Gióng leads.** He rides in front of the child, in the view, and waits at the turns. A child who does not tap the star can follow him: a person to follow is a landmark that goes with the child.
- **The lines say the truth.** The soldiers are at the foot of Trâu Sơn, far from the village. "Toward the sunrise" stays a clue of the story for the older children; the person who says it points along the road, and at night the line names the road too.
- **The map of the village shows the star of a far goal** on its edge.

## The limit of what we know

The two studies were in a room and in a maze on a computer, not in a game world with a guide, and the second one was only with children of 5. We do not know the best length of a walk for a child of K. We will look at the next play tests: the number of taps, the time of the walk, and what the children say on the road.
