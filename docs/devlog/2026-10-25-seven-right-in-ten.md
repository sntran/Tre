# Seven right in ten

*25 October 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-10-25-seven-right-in-ten.vi.md)*

After the Five Trials, the messenger of the Hùng King tests the child at the village hall, to find the level of math. In the play test, a girl of grade 4 got all 15 questions right and asked: "Is this a game or a worksheet?" The result said "about grade 6", and she asked: "now will everything be lớp 6 hard?" A boy of grade 3 got 6 of 10, two of them by a guess, and said: "I feel a bit dumb." The test did its work as a measure, and both children came out of it feeling worse. This entry tells why, and what we change.

## What we found

- **An adaptive test keeps a player at half right.** It chooses each question at the level where the player has about one chance in two, because that question gives the most information. So the player gets about half of the questions wrong, at every level. In a study with 174 students of 15 to 21 years, the adaptive form of a test of reasoning gave more fear of failure and a lower sense of success than the fixed form of the same test (Ortner, Weißkopf and Koch 2014).
- **An easier test costs little.** In the simulations of Eggen and Verschoor, a test that aims at 70 percent right needed about one question more than the best test, for the same precision. The cost grows fast above that: at 90 percent, 16 questions in place of 10 in one bank of questions.
- **Children do not skip.** In interviews with children of grade 5, many said that it is wrong to skip a question, partly because it can look like "not trying" (Childs, Elgie, Brijmohan and Yang 2019). Our boy of grade 3 guessed in place of a skip, and his guesses placed him one grade too high.
- **A grade is not information about the task.** Rules 16 and 42 of our design ask for information about the task and about what the child did. "About grade 6" did not tell the girl what she does well or what comes next. It told her that the next tasks would be hard.

## What we decided

- **At most 10 questions, aimed at about seven right in ten.** Each question of a new skill uses the smallest numbers that still test that skill (23 × 14, not 826 × 74), so that no question needs paper. Two questions of the same kind never come one after the other.
- **The answer after each question.** The messenger says if the answer is right, and when it is not, the right answer with one short line. Rule 26 of our design already asked for this for all questions; the placement test did not do it.
- **"Cháu chưa học bài này" is a choice of the messenger.** He says it on the first question: a question that the child has not learned yet gets this button, and that is fine. It counts as not known, with no guess, and the next question is easier.
- **The child hears what he can do, not a grade.** One thing done well and one thing to learn next, in the words of the task. The grade goes to the parent page, and never above grade 5, the top grade that has tasks now.
- **The calling that follows matches the child.** The card of each calling shows the child's own hero next to the mentor, and the task of that mentor at the grade of the child. In the play test, the line of the scholar was the task of grade 1, and the girl took the picture of the smith for her new look: "Is that supposed to be me? I'm a girl with long hair."

## The limit of what we know

The study of fear of failure was with older students and a test of reasoning, not with children of grade 3 and a test of math. The simulations of Eggen and Verschoor are about precision, not about how a child feels. So we will look at the next play tests: the time of the test, the part of right answers, and what the children say after it.
