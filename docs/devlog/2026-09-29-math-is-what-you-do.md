# Math is what you do, not a question

*29 September 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-09-29-math-is-what-you-do.vi.md)*

The first version of Tre had battles with questions: a problem came up, and the child picked an answer. When we played it, it felt like a test with pictures around it. So we asked a plain question: how do children of 5 to 12 learn math best in a game?

## What we found

- **Discovery with no help loses; guided doing wins.** Across many studies, children who discover with no help learn less than children who get direct teaching (d = −0.38). But guided discovery, with feedback and worked examples, wins (d = +0.30) ([Alfieri et al. 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ933606)).
- **When the math is the action, children learn more and play longer.** Children of 7 and 8 scored 58% on a later test when the math was the action of the game itself, and 41% when the same math came as quizzes at the end of a level. Older children chose to play the action version seven times longer ([Habgood & Ainsworth 2011](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/1/Habgood_Ainsworth_final.pdf)).
- **Feedback that shows why teaches much more than right or wrong.** Feedback on the task and the process has an effect of d = 0.99; a plain right-or-wrong mark has d = 0.24 to 0.46 ([Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)).

## What we decided

In the world of Tre there are no questions. The math is inside the work: ten counting rods tie into a bundle for the teacher, equal sticks for the woodcutter, enough planks to cross a gap. The world answers each try at once, and it shows why: the band snaps, the short stick breaks, the plank dips into the water. Nothing is lost by a try.

Questions stay in one place only: short, spaced practice with a teacher, and the exams of Văn Miếu.
