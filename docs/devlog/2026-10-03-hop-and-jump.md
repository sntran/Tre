# Hopscotch, jump rope, and a jump as far as a real child

*3 October 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-10-03-hop-and-jump.vi.md)*

We wanted children in the game to play nhảy lò cò (hopscotch) and nhảy dây (jump rope), as children do in Vietnamese villages. That needed a jump. We looked at three questions: does a jump in a game help learning, how far should a child jump, and how can these games carry math?

## What we found

- **The court of nhảy lò cò is a number line.** It has ten squares, 1 to 5 up one side and 6 to 10 down the other ([Du lịch Việt Nam](https://www.dulichvn.org.vn/index.php/item/14698)). A board game with a straight line of numbers improves young children's sense of numbers; a round board does not. **How the child counts matters:** kindergartners who counted on from their square improved on the number line, and those who counted from one at each move did not ([Laski & Siegler 2014](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/2014-Laski-Siegler.pdf)).
- **The real body teaches the most.** First graders (about 7 years old) who walked a number line on the floor learned more than with the same task without whole-body movement, and the gain carried over to addition ([Link et al. 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211949313000197)). A tap on a tablet is not that.
- **A real child's jump grows with age.** The usual standing long jump is about 75 cm at age 4 and about 110 to 120 cm at age 7 ([Polish children 4–7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-86903-x); [IDEFICS](https://www.nature.com/articles/ijo2014136)).
- **How old are these games?** Nobody knows. The first written record of hopscotch is from England in the 1600s, and the story of Roman soldiers who played it has no source ([Historic Game Rules](https://historicgamerules.com/hopscotch-when-an-ancient-game-isnt/)).

## What we decided

- **The hero jumps as far as a real child of the player's age,** with the same jump for girls and boys.
- **A jump never crosses a gap that a task asks the child to fill.** A jump over the gap of the broken bridge ends in the water, so the planks stay the way across.
- **In nhảy lò cò, the children say the number of each square as the hero lands on it,** so the child counts on. Older players get courts that start at bigger numbers or count by 2s, 5s, or 10s.
- **In nhảy dây, the chant counts by groups,** which is the base of multiplication.
- **The parent page suggests the real game:** draw the same court with chalk, and say the numbers out loud.
- **Folk games are part of village life in every era,** and the game says nothing about how old they are.
