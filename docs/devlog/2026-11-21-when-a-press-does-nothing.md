# When a press does nothing

*21 November 2026 · [Tiếng Việt](2026-11-21-when-a-press-does-nothing.vi.md)*

After #174 to #182, eight testers who did not know the game played short plays of 45 to 60 touches, each from a saved game at the scene of one change: two children of K, two of grade 1, two of grade 2, one of grade 3, and one of grade 4. Before the plays, the robot child of #174 checked each saved game, and it showed that two of my saves led to the wrong person. Many of the changes worked. A girl of K and a boy of grade 2 both crossed the river riddle, and each move of the boy was a move that the voice named. A boy of grade 4 found the recipe of the medicine, and the five packs of bà lang became a multiplication for him. But the children found a kind of problem that the robot did not look for: a press that does nothing.

## What we found

- **A press that does nothing looks like a wrong answer.** A boy of grade 4 tapped a bed of leaves, and his hero walked. He pressed seven times during the walk, and nothing happened: "I pressed the button 6 times really fast and she didn't count anything!" A girl of grade 1 held the big button for three seconds because the voice said "Em giữ nút lớn", and the knife was not on the button yet. Norman (2013) puts immediate feedback first: an action with no response looks like a broken control.
- **One work at a time on the button.** At the share of the rice after a raid, the button turned into the slingshot of the next raid. A girl of grade 2 pressed it ("Are soldiers coming? I don't see any."), and the raid started again in the middle of the share. That was the moment she got upset.
- **A cue needs time after the words.** At the forge, the iron bar stays red for 1.4 seconds at grade 3, and the line "Đỏ rồi! Nhúng ngay!" takes about 1.2 seconds to say. A boy of grade 3 pressed right after the line and heard "Sắt nguội mất rồi". Then each new press bent the cold bar again, three times in a row. Kail (1991) found that children of eight process and answer about one and a half to two times as slowly as adults.
- **A see-through soldier is a ghost.** To keep the posts of the road in view, a soldier in front of a post was drawn see-through. Three children of K to grade 2 asked the same thing: "Is the ghost not the real one?" Elmqvist and Tsigas (2008) found that a see-through layer weakens the cues of depth; for a child of five it also changes what the thing is.
- **The goal before the try.** A girl of grade 1 cut a bamboo staff of six rings, because nobody had said that the sample has four. The boy at bà lang found what one pack holds only with an accidental tap. Sweller and Cooper (1985) found that learners who study a worked example first solve the next problems faster.
- **What the child carried was gone.** After each crossing of the river, the dog, the chicken, or the rice was not on the screen by the hero, though the game had them there: "Where did the chicken go?"
- **Our own test phone had an error again.** The big button now names its target, and our test phone did not know the new name, so each press counted as 2.5 seconds of the game. The first trap of the boy of K went to the wrong post for that reason. We fixed the phone and played again.

## What we decided

- **The robot child learns the new dead ends** (#183): one lost press, the act of another work on the button, the same fail three times with presses only, a line that names the button before the button does it, and a thing of a puzzle that the screen does not draw.
- **A press is never lost** (#184): a press during a walk does its act at the end of the walk, a press from away walks to a commit and stops there, and the button never changes under the finger.
- **The share stays the share, and the raid waits for the trap up to grade 2** (#185). **The soldiers stay solid, and the ring of a count and the line agree** (#186).
- **The number of the sample before the first cut** (#187), **a window of seconds after "Nhúng ngay!" and no loop of bends** (#188), **the recipe before the first put** (#189), **the things on both banks in view** (#190), and smaller lines and loops (#191).

## The limit of what we know

These were eight short plays on test phones, by testers who did not know the game, and we heard them through their notes. Two of the saves still put the child in a later raid than the one we wanted, so some notes come from a second raid. Some of the late dip at the forge came from our test phone, which let the world run on during a wait. The rule of the window of a cue comes from studies of the speed of children in general, not from a test of this forge.
